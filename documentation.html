[01] Data Source / Aquascope Eawag / Lake Greifensee

Over 60 million images of the world beneath the surface of Lake Greifensee have been taken and observed by the Aquascope research group at Eawag. This growing archive forms one of the most comprehensive datasets ever created of this largely unseen world [A]

Every hour, for ten minutes, an underwater microscope records what drifts past it [B], and a neural network sorts each image into classes of plankton [C]. The resulting counts are published by Eawag as a public dataset [D] and updated as new hours come in.

Current’s Wanderers reads the latest data from the lake. Each cell below shows how often a class was detected, as individuals per second averaged over the hour.

[02] From Data to Image / Frequency, Size and Flow

In the installation, every class the Aquascope counts becomes a creature: an animation drawn for the project. There are 52 of them, because a few classes have more than one variant and take turns. They are numbered as in the software.

The data decides when a creature appears. The installation reads the most recent record and, sixty times a second, gives each class a chance to be born: its frequency divided by sixty. A class seen 0.2 times per second appears about once every five seconds; a class that was not seen never appears. A creature enters from the right edge at a random height, is carried by a flow field and is gone when it leaves the frame. At most fifty are alive at once.

The biology decides how big it is. The size follows the longest axis of the organism, from 5 µm to 12,000 µm, on a logarithmic scale: the smallest stays visible at 5%, the largest fills the frame at 100%.

Size on screen
scale = 5 + 95 × (log10 x − log10 5) / (log10 12000 − log10 5)
