[01] Data Source / Aquascope Eawag / Lake Greifensee

Over 60 million images of the world beneath the surface of Lake Greifensee have been taken and observed by the Aquascope research group at Eawag. This growing archive forms one of the most comprehensive datasets ever created of this largely unseen world [A]

Every hour, for ten minutes, an underwater microscope records what drifts past it [B], and a neural network sorts each image into classes of plankton [C]. The resulting counts are published by Eawag as a public dataset [D] and updated as new hours come in.

Current’s Wanderers reads the latest data from the lake. Each cell below shows how often a class was detected, as individuals per second averaged over the hour. The tag next to a name says which of the microscope’s two lenses saw it: 0.5× for larger organisms, from about 0.1 mm to 1 cm, and 5× for smaller ones, from about 10 to 150 µm [E]

[02] From Data to Image / Frequency, Size and Flow

In the installation, every class the Aquascope counts becomes a creature: an animation drawn for the project. There are 52 of them, because a few classes have more than one variant and take turns. They are numbered as in the software.

This is not the square above. That one is a snapshot: one sphere for every individual counted in the latest hour, all drifting together. The installation works as a stream instead: creatures are born one at a time, at the rhythm the data gives them.

The data decides when a creature appears. The installation reads the most recent record and, sixty times a second, gives each class a chance to be born: its frequency divided by sixty. A class seen 0.2 times per second appears about once every five seconds; a class that was not seen never appears. Nothing is sped up: a frequent class keeps arriving every few seconds, while the rarest class still detected appears only once every 10 to 40 minutes, so a visitor may never meet it. A creature enters from the right edge at a random height, is carried by a flow field, described in the next chapter, and is gone when it leaves the frame. At most fifty are alive at once.

The biology decides how big it is. The size follows the longest axis of the organism, from 5 µm to 12,000 µm, on a logarithmic scale: the smallest stays visible at 5%, the largest fills the frame at 100%.

Size on screen
scale = 5 + 95 × (log10 x − log10 5) / (log10 12000 − log10 5)

[03] Flow and Touch / Hands as Currents

The creatures do not move on their own: they are carried by a flow field, a grid of 20 px cells, 96 by 96 over the 1920 px canvas, each holding a direction. The directions come from Perlin noise that slowly changes in time, so the current meanders and never repeats. A creature turns to follow the direction of the cell it is in.

Hands change the current. The installation tracks both hands, a palm and five fingertips each, twelve points in all. What a point does to the current depends on whether it moves and for how long it has been still; the three behaviours are listed below.

Below, the same logic runs in the browser. Move the pointer over the square: it stands in for one fingertip, or for a pair of hands with the switch above. The faint lines show the direction of the current in each cell, and brighten where a point is steering it. The speed-up of creatures near a fast point is toned down here compared with the installation.

Flow field
Cells of 20 px, 96 × 96. Perlin noise evolving slowly in time, a little faster when a point moves fast.

Influence of a moving point
Radius from 50 to 500 px, from its speed (0 to 40 px per frame).

Creature speed, in px per frame
Free flow: 6
A third of the points held: 3 · two thirds: 1
Next to a held point: 0.5
Next to a fast hand: up to 710

Moving
A point that moves turns the current around it to follow its direction. The faster it goes, the wider the area it steers, from 50 to 500 px, and the creatures inside that area are swept along, up to very high speeds.

Still, briefly
A point that has just stopped only pushes the current gently into a swirl around it, within 40 px.

Held
After two seconds without moving, the current starts to bend towards the point, over a radius that grows from 40 to 200 px, and the creatures close to it slow almost to a stop. The more points are held, the slower all the other creatures become.

[04] Haptic Gloves and Arduino / From Contact to Vibration

Plankton cannot be heard, smelled or touched. The gloves add the missing sense: when a creature on the screen is also felt on the hand, the two read as one contact. In an early test with two daphnia on the screen, only one of them wired to a vibrating motor, the visitor’s attention went almost entirely to that one.

Every frame, the software checks each of the twelve points tracked on the two hands, the palm and the five fingertips of each. A point is in contact when it is within 150 px of a living creature. For every point in contact, TouchDesigner sends the Arduino one line of text: the pin that drives that point’s motor, a colon and a power from 0 to 255, for instance 8:27. While the contact lasts a new value is sent every frame; when it ends, the pin gets a single 0. The Arduino reads each line and sets that pin to the power it was given: through an NMOS transistor on a breadboard, the pin drives two coin motors wired in parallel, so each glove has twelve motors and the pair has twenty-four. For safety, each glove also has a resistor.

The power is never constant. It oscillates around 25, a little either side, along a slow wave with some noise, and every pin has its own phase. The first prototype, a coin motor on each fingertip of a cycling glove built after a public tutorial [F], showed that the vibrations felt most alive close to the minimum power at which the motors start, almost like a living body, so that is where they are kept.

Below, the same rule runs in the browser. Move the pointer over the creatures: it moves a pair of hands, twelve points, mirrored about the middle. When a point touches a creature, its channel lights up in the diagram, its two motors shake, and the line that would go to the Arduino appears in the log.

Serial link
USB serial, 500,000 baud. One line per pin, pin:power, power from 0 to 255. Each line is sent twice, to avoid a problem in which the Arduino blocked the vibration of a pin.

Contact
A point within 150 px (on the 1920 px canvas) of a living creature.

Circuit
The Arduino’s 5 V and ground feed the rails of the breadboard. Each pin goes to the gate of an NMOS transistor, which switches the ground of its two motors. A safety resistor protects each glove.

Power value
About 25 while in contact, with a slow wave and a little noise. 0 otherwise.

Pins
Right glove: palm 10 · thumb 9 · index 8 · middle 13 · ring 12 · pinky 11
Left glove: palm 4 · thumb 3 · index 2 · middle 7 · ring 6 · pinky 5
