[01] Data Source / Aquascope Eawag / Lake Greifensee

Over 60 million images of the world beneath the surface of Lake Greifensee have been taken and observed by the Aquascope research group at Eawag. This growing archive forms one of the most comprehensive datasets ever created of this largely unseen world [A]

Every hour, for ten minutes, an underwater microscope records what drifts past it [B], and a neural network sorts each image into classes of plankton [C]. The resulting counts are published by Eawag as a public dataset [D] and updated as new hours come in.

Current’s Wanderers reads the latest data from the lake. Each cell below shows how often a class was detected, as individuals per second averaged over the hour. The tag next to a name says which of the microscope’s two lenses saw it: 0.5× for larger organisms, from about 0.1 mm to 1 cm, and 5× for smaller ones, from about 10 to 150 µm [E]

[A] Pomati, Francesco. 2025. ‘Instant Insights: Real-Time Plankton Classification Now Online – AQUASCOPE’. aquascope.ch/2025/08/21/diving-deep-real-time-plankton-monitoring-in-greifensee [B] Zierl, Bärbel. 2020. ‘Aquascope: Shedding Light on Underwater Life’. eawag.ch/en/info/portal/news/news-detail/aquascope-shedding-light-on-underwater-life [C] Kyathanahally, Sreenath P., Thomas Hardeman, Ewa Merz, et al. 2021. ‘Deep Learning Classification of Lake Zooplankton’. Frontiers in Microbiology 12 (November): 746297. doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2021.746297 [D] Eawag · Aquascope. ‘Lake Greifen Aquascope’, dataset 1387, Datalakes. datalakes-eawag.ch/datadetail/1387 [E] Merz, Ewa, Thea Kozakiewicz, Marta Reyes, et al. 2021. ‘Underwater Dual-Magnification Imaging for Automated Lake Plankton Monitoring’. [02] From Data to Image / Frequency, Size and Flow

In the installation, every class the Aquascope counts becomes a creature: an animation drawn for the project. There are 52 of them, because a few classes have more than one variant and take turns. They are numbered as in the software.

This is not the square above. That one is a snapshot: one sphere for every individual counted in the latest hour, all drifting together. The installation works as a stream instead: creatures are born one at a time, at the rhythm the data gives them.

The data decides when a creature appears. The installation reads the most recent record and, sixty times a second, gives each class a chance to be born: its frequency divided by sixty. A class seen 0.2 times per second appears about once every five seconds; a class that was not seen never appears. Nothing is sped up: a frequent class keeps arriving every few seconds, while the rarest class still detected appears only once every 10 to 40 minutes, so a visitor may never meet it. A creature enters from the right edge at a random height, is carried by a flow field, described in the next chapter, and is gone when it leaves the frame. At most fifty are alive at once.

The biology decides how big it is. The size follows the longest axis of the organism, from 5 µm to 12,000 µm, on a logarithmic scale: the smallest stays visible at 5%, the largest fills the frame at 100%.

Size on screen
scale = 5 + 95 × (log10 x − log10 5) / (log10 12000 − log10 5)

x: longest axis of the organism, in µm

Smallest: Chroococcales, 5 µm → 5%
Largest: Leptodora, 12000 µm → 100%

The 52 creatures of the installation, numbered as in the software.

Brighter: the class was detected in the latest hour. Fainter: it was not, so it would not appear.

[03] Flow and Touch / Hands as Currents

The creatures do not move on their own: they are carried by a flow field, a grid of 20 px cells, 96 by 96 over the 1920 px canvas, each holding a direction. The directions come from Perlin noise that slowly changes in time, so the current meanders and never repeats. A creature turns to follow the direction of the cell it is in.

Hands change the current. The installation tracks both hands, a palm and five fingertips each, twelve points in all. What a point does to the current depends on whether it moves and for how long it has been still; the three behaviours are listed below.

Below, the same logic runs in the browser. Move the pointer over the square: it stands in for one fingertip. The faint lines show the direction of the current in each cell, and brighten where a point is steering it. The speed-up of creatures near a fast point is toned down here compared with the installation.

Flow field
Cells of 20 px, 96 × 96. Perlin noise evolving slowly in time, a little faster when a point moves fast.

Influence of a moving point
Radius from 50 to 500 px, from its speed (0 to 40 px per frame).

Creature speed, in px per frame
Free flow: 6
A third of the points held: 3 · two thirds: 1
Next to a held point: 0.5
Next to a fast hand: up to 710

Moving
A point that moves turns the current around it to follow its direction. The faster it goes, the wider the area it steers, from 50 to 500 px, and the creatures inside that area are swept along, up to very high speeds.

Still, briefly
A point that has just stopped only pushes the current gently into a swirl around it, within 40 px.

Held
After two seconds without moving, the current starts to bend towards the point, over a radius that grows from 40 to 200 px, and the creatures close to it slow almost to a stop. The more points are held, the slower all the other creatures become.

[04] Hand Tracking / The Leap Motion, Simulated

The hands are followed by a Leap Motion, an infrared sensor in the middle of the cube, under the hands. A small Python script, the bridge, reads it and sends every hand to TouchDesigner as OSC messages on port 7000: the palm, the fingers and other joints, their positions in millimetres, whether the hand is visible, and gestures such as grab and pinch.

TouchDesigner keeps twelve of those points, the palm and the five fingertips of each hand, and places them on the 1920 px canvas: the position from side to side, in millimetres, is multiplied by 3.2, the depth from +200 to −200 mm runs across the height of the canvas, and the height of the hands scales the whole area, from 1.5 times when they are low to 0.3 times when they are high.

Below, the sensor is replaced by the camera of your computer. On the left, the picture as an infrared sensor would give it: mirrored and in black and white. On the right, on a square of the same size, the joints that MediaPipe, a hand-tracking model by Google [G], finds on each hand (on a narrow screen the two squares lie one over the other, the picture very dark). The camera is switched on only if you choose it, and its picture never leaves your computer.

Messages
OSC, port 7000: /leap/{left|right}/… palm, fingers, arm, grab, pinch.

Points kept
The palm and the five fingertips of each hand: 12.

Canvas
x = 960 + 3.2 × x (mm). Depth +200 … −200 mm → 0 … 1920 px.

Scale
Height 50 mm → 1.5 · 500 mm → 0.3, around the middle of the canvas.

[05] Haptic Gloves and Arduino / From Contact to Vibration

Plankton cannot be heard, smelled or touched. The gloves add the missing sense: when a creature on the screen is also felt on the hand, the two read as one contact. In an early test with two daphnia on the screen, only one of them wired to a vibrating motor, the visitor’s attention went almost entirely to that one.

Every frame, the software checks each of the twelve points tracked on the two hands, the palm and the five fingertips of each. A point is in contact when it is within 150 px of a living creature. For every point in contact, TouchDesigner sends the Arduino one line of text: the pin that drives that point’s motor, a colon and a power from 0 to 255, for instance 8:27. While the contact lasts a new value is sent every frame; when it ends, the pin gets a single 0. The Arduino reads each line and sets that pin to the power it was given: through an NMOS transistor on a breadboard, the pin drives two coin motors wired in parallel, so each glove has twelve motors and the pair has twenty-four. For safety, every pin goes through a resistor on its way to the transistor.

The power is never constant. It oscillates around 25, a little either side, along a slow wave with some noise, and every pin has its own phase. The first prototype, a coin motor on each fingertip of a cycling glove built after a public tutorial [F], showed that the vibrations felt most alive close to the minimum power at which the motors start, almost like a living body, so that is where they are kept.

Below, the same rule runs in the browser, with your own hands. Switch the camera on and hold them in front of it: MediaPipe, as in the previous chapter, finds the twelve points, and the square is the picture of the camera, mirrored. When a point touches a creature, its channel lights up in the diagram, its two motors shake, and the line that would go to the Arduino appears in the log.

Serial link
USB serial, 500,000 baud. One line per pin, pin:power, power from 0 to 255. Each line is sent twice, to avoid a problem in which the Arduino blocked the vibration of a pin.

Contact
A point within 150 px (on the 1920 px canvas) of a living creature.

Circuit
The Arduino’s 5 V and ground feed the rails of the breadboard. Each pin goes to the gate of an NMOS transistor, which switches the ground of its two motors. A safety resistor sits on every pin, before the transistor.

Power value
About 25 while in contact, with a slow wave and a little noise. 0 otherwise.

Pins
Right glove: palm 10 · thumb 9 · index 8 · middle 13 · ring 12 · pinky 11
Left glove: palm 4 · thumb 3 · index 2 · middle 7 · ring 6 · pinky 5

[06] The Network / The Installation Patch in TouchDesigner

Everything described in this documentation runs in a single TouchDesigner project. Below is its network as it stood when the installation was shown: 392 nodes joined by 270 connections, redrawn as a map to move around in. It is a reference, not a tutorial, and it keeps the original layout.

Much of it is repetition. Every creature has a movie node and a level node, and the creatures that make a sound have an audio node and an audio output as well, which is why the long rows of identical nodes are there. The rest is the logic of the previous chapters: the data and its JSON, the Python scripts that give birth to the creatures and compute the flow, the Leap Motion input, the serial link to the Arduino, and the shaders that draw the final image.

Drag to move around. Pinch, or hold Ctrl or ⌘ and scroll, to zoom. Move the pointer over a node to follow its connections (a click keeps it). The groups, like the families, light up the parts of the patch that do one job while the pointer is on them (a click keeps them lit), and the entries and exits mark what comes into the patch from outside (the data, the hands, the files) and where it leaves (the Arduino, the projectors, the speakers); hover one of them to see every node it reaches.

Families
Each family has its own outline: plain, dashed, shaded, double, dotted or heavy on top. Click one to light up all its nodes.

Connections
They run from left to right: outputs on the right of a node, inputs on its left. Only direct wires are drawn, not the links a script makes by naming another node.

Not drawn
The inside of the components (geometries, cameras, lights, windows).

[07] The Structure / The Cube of the Installation

The installation is a cube of 60 × 60 × 60 cm, a stand-in for the cubic centimetre of water that the underwater microscope looks at. It is built like a piece of laboratory equipment: a frame of aluminium profiles of 2 × 2 cm, side panels of 4 mm frosted acrylic, and painted MDF for the top and the bottom. It stands on four legs of the same profile, with its bottom about 40 cm from the floor.

The top surface works like a glovebox. Two openings lead to a pair of gloves hanging inside, so that the visitor’s hands disappear into the cube, and a long-throw projector hung from the ceiling draws the visualisation on that surface, together with a digital version of the hands.

Everything else is hidden. A wooden box, laser-cut, holds a Mac Studio and carries the Leap Motion in the middle of the cube, 40 cm below the top surface and 20 cm above the bottom one. On the floor of the cube sit the Arduino and its two breadboards. Around it, in a corner of the room, the projected surface of the lake covers two walls, much larger than the cube and above it, the projector is hung high from the ceiling, and a pair of loudspeakers stands on the floor under the cube. Visitors wear headphones.

Below, the whole installation is a model to turn: press and drag to rotate it, move the pointer over a part to light it up, pull it apart, and switch each layer on or off.

Cube
600 × 600 × 600 mm, on four legs: its bottom is about 400 mm from the floor. Frame of aluminium profiles, 20 × 20 mm.

Panels
Frosted acrylic, 4 mm, on the sides. Painted MDF, 555 × 555 mm, for the top and the bottom.

Openings
Two of Ø 150 mm in the top surface, 340 mm centre to centre.

Sensor
Leap Motion, in the middle of the cube: 400 mm below the top surface, 200 mm above the bottom one.

Box
Wood, laser-cut, 300 × 300 × 200 mm. It holds the computer and the sensor.
